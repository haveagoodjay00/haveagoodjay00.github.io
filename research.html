Stage 1 of 4 completed

Large loads on a transmission network: a staged study on the IEEE 24-bus Reliability Test System

I'm studying how much room a transmission network has for a large new load such as a data center, in four stages. The first stage is complete and is reported in detail below.

Motivation

This study started from a single question. I see engineering as work that makes people's lives a little more convenient and more harmonious. As the AI industry grows, data centers are being built quickly, and there are continuing reports of nearby households facing problems such as less reliable power supply, outages and noise. Instead of responding after problems appear, could they be prevented at the planning and design stage? This study takes up the power-grid side of that question: when a large data center load is connected, can the existing grid stay within its limits and keep a margin? Other issues, such as noise, are outside its scope.

Stage 1 of 4, completed November 2026

Operating margin for a data center load at one bus

Summary Question Model Checks Result 1 Result 2 Result 3 Limits Method and role

Summary

  • Question: with generator settings fixed for a data center of about 100 MW at bus 4, does the network stay within its limits as demand changes?
  • Result 1: only one of four candidate settings (P1Q1) met all three criteria at 95, 100 and 105 MW.
  • Result 2: over 17 demand levels from 90 to 110 MW, 13 passed and 4 broke a physical limit, the first at 106.25 MW.
  • Result 3: enforcing generator reactive limits removes the violation at 106.25 MW but still leaves no margin.

The sections below give the model, criteria and numbers. The stage 1 report and code will be on GitHub once I have finished reviewing them for public release.

The question

I use a data center as the test case for a general operating question: when a large load is added, how much margin is left before the network reaches its limits?

A power-flow calculation that converges only tells you the equations were solved. It does not tell you that every generator and line stayed inside its rating, and it says nothing about how close to the edge the system is running. I kept those three questions separate throughout.

Model

  • Network: the IEEE 24-bus Reliability Test System, a public benchmark.
  • Data center: a constant-power load at bus 4, with reactive demand assumed to be 0.25 times active demand.
  • Generation: 22 fixed injections keep their input values. Eleven sources respond: ten generators and the reference source at bus 13.
  • Control: each generator's active power and voltage target are held fixed, and its reactive power responds. The reference source balances active power, losses included.

These are modelling assumptions on a test network, not measurements from a real data center or a real grid.

Three checks

CheckWhat it asksThreshold used here
ConvergenceDid the solver reach a solution?Recorded separately from the limits below
Criterion AAre the original physical limits respected?Generator P and Q, bus voltages, line and transformer ratings, power balance
Criterion BIs there reactive and voltage margin left?At least 0.25 Mvar on every responding source and 0.001 pu on every bus
Criterion CIs there active-power margin on the reference source?At least 1 MW from either limit

The margins in B and C are buffers I chose for this study. They are not a regulatory requirement or a guarantee of operating reserve.

How I read this

Only P1Q1, which narrows both ranges, passed at all three levels. Narrowing one range is not enough: at 105 MW, P1Q0 leaves 0.1833 Mvar of reactive margin (buffer 0.25), and P0Q1 pushes the reference source to 202.494 MW (limit 197).

Active and reactive headroom are separate design decisions, and here both were needed. One candidate per setting, so this describes four cases, not a general rule. Criterion C was added after P0Q0 was run.

Result 2: one setting, seventeen demand levels

With P1Q1 fixed, I checked 17 preset demand levels from 90 to 110 MW in 1.25 MW steps. 13 passed; the last 4 broke at least one physical limit.

Gen 1 (bus 2) adds about 0.5 Mvar per step to hold its voltage. At 106.25 MW it needs 10.2557 Mvar against a 10 Mvar limit: the first violation.

The reference source absorbs the added demand and losses almost linearly. It enters the 1 MW buffer at 106.25 MW (196.869 MW) and passes the 197 MW limit at 107.5 MW (198.246 MW). Violations then accumulate: 1/3/4/6 across the last four points.

How I read this

It passes at 105 MW, but only just: 0.2566 Mvar of reactive margin (buffer 0.25) and 0.001000 pu of voltage margin (buffer 0.001). A setting optimized for one demand level sits close to its limits and does not carry far.

Seventeen separate points: this does not prove every level in between passes, and 105 MW is not a maximum capacity.

Result 3: enforcing reactive limits

With reactive-limit enforcement on, a generator that hits its Q limit is pinned there and stops holding its voltage (PV to PQ). I ran the same inputs with the option off and on.

How I read this

At 105.5 MW nothing reaches a limit, so both rows match. At 106.25 MW the option pins Gen 1 at 10 Mvar and the violation disappears, but bus 2 voltage drops slightly below target and B and C still fail: a generator on its limit has no margin.

Removing a violation is not passing. PV to PQ switching is a rule in a static calculation, not a model of a real voltage regulator over time.

What stage 1 does not show

The original goal was a data center hosting study that includes single-contingency (N-1) security. The first calculations showed that the base network violated normal-operation limits even without the data center, so Stage 1 first established and evaluated operating settings that respect limits and margins under normal conditions. What Stage 1 does not show (N-1 security, hosting capacity, other locations) became the research questions for the later stages.

  • N-1 security. Outages were screened earlier for selected cases only. This is stage 2.
  • A maximum hosting capacity, or a continuous range of acceptable demand. This is stage 3.
  • Other locations. The data center stays at bus 4 throughout. This is stage 4.
  • Dynamic behaviour. Every result is a steady-state snapshot.
  • A real system. Open questions about some benchmark reactive limits were inherited and not resolved.

How it was done

Stage 1 shows 33 stored results that come from 29 unique power-flow runs, since four results are reused between sections. The four optimal power flows that produced the settings are counted separately. Tools: Python with pandapower, and MATPOWER in MATLAB.

I set the direction, scope and pass or fail criteria and reviewed the results. ChatGPT supported planning and review; comparing several operating settings was its suggestion, which I adopted. Claude Code and Codex implemented and ran the calculations. I did not write all of the code by hand.

For the full report, code, run records and the journal of each decision, see my GitHub once the repository is public. This page keeps to the results.
